Combined texts, quarter by quarter

What to watch for in each quarter — the vocabulary to master, the genre moves to name, and the questions to hold while you read. Laid out as Cornell notes so you can mirror the format in your own notebook.

First, a definition: what is a field of inquiry?

A field of inquiry is one of five broad areas the IB uses to sort the big human questions that texts raise. Think of them as the five shelves any global issue can sit on:

  • Culture, identity and community who belongs, who is excluded, and what identity costs
  • Beliefs, values and education what a society holds true and passes on
  • Politics, power and justice who has power, and how it is used or resisted
  • Art, creativity and the imagination what art is for, and who decides
  • Science, technology and the environment people, technology, and the natural world

A field is not your global issue — it is far too big for that. Your global issue is a narrow, arguable claim you carve out of a field: it must matter beyond one text, be visible in the text itself, and be provable in ten minutes.

Field: Politics, power and justice.   Too broad: “racism in Othello.”   A global issue: “how a society lets an outsider succeed while never letting him belong, so that his identity stays something others assign him.”

The Individual Oral opens by naming your field, then narrowing to your issue. Practise that move all year — see the ladder in the question banks.

Q1

Time & Space

Texts

  • Othello — Shakespeare
  • Five speeches — Truth to Thunberg
  • Political cartoons — Bellew, Harper’s Weekly

Focus terms & genre elements

Drama
soliloquy
a character alone on stage, thinking aloud to the audience
aside
a remark the audience hears but other characters do not
dramatic irony
the audience knows what a character does not
blank verse
unrhymed iambic pentameter — Shakespeare’s default rhythm
tragic arc
the fall of a figure of stature, driven by a flaw or a manipulation
stagecraft
entrances, exits, props and staging as meaning
Oratory
ethos
persuasion by the speaker’s character or standing
pathos
persuasion through the audience’s emotion
logos
persuasion by reasoning and evidence
anaphora
repeating the same opening words across clauses (‘I have a dream…’)
apostrophe
turning to address someone absent, or an idea
cadence
the rhythm of delivery; how sentences land aloud
Political cartoon
caricature
exaggerating a feature to make an argument about a person
visual metaphor
an image standing in for an idea
labelling
writing on figures or objects to fix their meaning
ventriloquism
putting invented speech in someone else’s mouth
juxtaposition
placing two images together so one judges the other

Genre characteristics

  • Tragedy — a persuasive villain and a protagonist talked into ruin
  • Oratory — argument built to be heard once, in a room, and remembered
  • Graphic satire — caricature making an argument no caption would admit to

Hold these questions while you read

  1. Track Iago’s method across three scenes: what does he state outright, what does he imply, and what does he leave Othello to supply himself?
  2. Count Othello’s lines before and after Act 3. What happens to his language as he loses control of the story?
  3. In each speech, where does the speaker claim authority — from position, from suffering, or from evidence?
  4. Find the sentence in each speech that was built to be quoted. What makes it portable?
  5. In the Bellew cartoon: who appears to be speaking, who actually drew the speech, and for whom?
  6. What must a viewer already believe for that cartoon to work? What does that tell you about its audience?

Working toward your global issue

  1. Iago and Dr King use recognisably similar techniques. What separates them — and can you say it without simply calling one good and one evil?
  2. In each text, who is permitted to speak, who is spoken about, and who is silent?
  3. Name the field of inquiry, then narrow it. Check that your statement would survive being read aloud to someone who disagrees.
Q2

Readers, Writers, and Texts

Texts

  • The Hour of the Star — Lispector
  • Advertisements — Nike to Ad*Access
  • Infographics — Du Bois, Our World in Data
  • Satire — Swift, Twain, Key & Peele

Focus terms & genre elements

Prose fiction
metafiction
fiction that draws attention to its own making
narrator intrusion
the narrator breaking in to comment on the telling
irony
a gap between what is said and what is meant
fragmentation
a text assembled from pieces rather than a smooth line
Advertising
target audience
the specific person the advertisement imagines
aspirational address
selling who you might become, not what the product does
slogan
a compressed, memorable claim
brand voice
the consistent personality a company speaks in
visual hierarchy
what the design makes you see first, second, last
call to action
the instruction: buy, join, share, believe
Data visualisation
axis
the measured edge of a chart; where it starts shapes the story
baseline
the zero point — moving it can exaggerate or flatten change
scale
the intervals chosen, and what they make look large or small
selection
what was counted, and what was left out
colour coding
colour used to group, rank or alarm
chart type
bar, line, map — each argues differently
Satire
persona
the invented speaker the satirist hides behind
hyperbole
deliberate exaggeration
understatement
deliberate flatness where outrage is expected
parody
imitating a form in order to mock it
target
who or what the satire is actually aimed at — often not the obvious one

Genre characteristics

  • Metafictional novella — a narrator who shows you the writing as he does it
  • Advertisement — persuasion that must not look like persuasion
  • Data visualisation — argument disguised as description
  • Satirical essay / sketch — a persona whose composure is the real subject

Hold these questions while you read

  1. Rodrigo interrupts himself constantly. What does each interruption protect him from having to do?
  2. What does the narrator insist he cannot do — and does he do it anyway?
  3. For each advertisement: what problem is invented so that the product can solve it?
  4. What kind of person is each advertisement inviting you to become? Who is left out of that invitation?
  5. For each infographic: where does the axis start, what was counted, and what could not be counted and so was dropped?
  6. In “A Modest Proposal,” find the first place the mask slips. How long can you read before you are certain?

Working toward your global issue

  1. Every text here represents someone to an audience. In each case, who benefits from the representation — and who pays for it?
  2. Set Du Bois’s data portraits beside the Bellew cartoon from Q1. What changes when the people being represented are the ones doing the representing?
  3. Name the field of inquiry, then narrow it until it is about a specific mechanism rather than a general topic.
Q3

Intertextuality / Review

Texts

  • Websites & blogs — NASA, NOAA, HONY, The Pudding
  • Review of all six works

Focus terms & genre elements

Digital text
hyperlink structure
what a page links to; the argument its connections make
navigation hierarchy
what the menu puts first, and what it buries
scroll rhetoric
how meaning is revealed by scrolling — pace, surprise, sequence
user address
how the site speaks to its visitor: ‘you’, ‘we’, or nobody
credibility markers
citations, logos, dates and design that signal trustworthiness
curation
choosing and ordering others’ material as an act of authorship
interface
the designed surface between reader and content

Genre characteristics

  • Institutional website — authority built through structure, citation and restraint
  • Photo-and-caption blog — a stranger’s life delivered in a paragraph
  • Visual essay — data journalism descended directly from Du Bois

Hold these questions while you read

  1. What does each site’s navigation put first, and what does it bury three clicks down?
  2. How does each site build credibility — citation, visual design, tone, or sheer volume?
  3. In a Humans of New York post: whose words are in the caption, and who chose where to crop the photograph?
  4. Set a HONY post beside a Lange photograph. What has changed about consent? What has not?
  5. Who is the imagined visitor to each site, and what are they meant to do before they leave?
  6. Return to all six works. Which one has changed most for you since you first read it, and what changed it?

Working toward your global issue

  1. Does new technology change the ethics of representing a stranger, or only the scale and speed?
  2. Across all six works: which one would you choose to defend under exam pressure, and on what grounds?
  3. Name the field of inquiry. Then find the text in this course that would most complicate your claim — and read it again.
Q4

Exam Preparation

Texts

  • All six works
  • All ten bodies of work
  • Paper 1 & Paper 2

Focus terms & genre elements

Analysis & assessment
guiding question
the question you build a Paper 1 analysis around
line of inquiry
the question driving an HL Essay — open, arguable, yours
comparative frame
the shared problem that lets two works be discussed together
textual evidence
the specific words or features you can point at
authorial choice
treating every feature as a decision someone made
text type conventions
what readers expect of this form — and what breaking it signals

Genre characteristics

  • Guided textual analysis — one unseen text, one guiding question, under time
  • Comparative essay — two works, one question, an argument neither makes alone

Hold these questions while you read

  1. For any unseen text, in sixty seconds: what type, what audience, what does it want from them?
  2. What is the single most unusual formal choice on the page? Could you build a whole essay from it?
  3. What does the text do at its start and at its end — and why in that order?
  4. For Paper 2: what problem do your two works share? Not theme — problem.
  5. Where do the two works diverge in method while agreeing in conclusion, or the reverse?
  6. Which work resists the question you have been asked, and can you say so intelligently rather than avoiding it?

Working toward your global issue

  1. Which field of inquiry could you argue for every one of the six works? Which could you argue for only one?
  2. Which field is your weakest? That is this quarter’s work.
  3. For each of the six works, write one sentence: the global issue it carries best, and the single piece of evidence you would reach for first.
