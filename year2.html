Why only two. Four of the six works are read in Year 1 so that every student has a full menu of texts to choose from for the Individual Oral in spring 2027. Year 2 adds Othello and The Hour of the Star, then spends its weight on Paper 1, Paper 2 and the HL Essay — all of which draw on all six.

Combined texts, quarter by quarter

How the literary and non-literary texts in each quarter speak to each other — the terms to master, the genre moves to name, the global issue they share, and the ones each text carries alone. Laid out as Cornell notes so you can mirror the format in your own notebook.

Q1

Time & Space

Texts

  • Othello — Shakespeare
  • Five speeches — Truth to Thunberg
  • Political cartoons — Bellew, Harper’s Weekly

Focus terms

  • rhetoric
  • ethos / pathos / logos
  • anaphora
  • soliloquy
  • dramatic irony
  • caricature
  • visual metaphor
  • ventriloquism
  • apostrophe

Genre characteristics

  • Tragedy — a persuasive villain and a protagonist talked into ruin
  • Oratory — argument built to be heard once, in a room, and remembered
  • Graphic satire — caricature making an argument no caption would admit to

How these texts connect

Every text here is about persuasion and who is permitted to speak. Iago persuades a general to destroy his own life. Douglass, Truth and King persuade a society that has excluded them, in that society’s own borrowed rhetoric. Bellew ventriloquises a Chinese voice for a white readership that never asked one.

The through-line is that the same instrument serves opposite ends. Iago’s technique — insinuation, repetition, the withheld accusation — is technically the same as King’s anaphora. The difference is what it is aimed at. That distinction is the most transferable thing in Year 2.

Teach the Bellew cartoon critically: it is racist caricature, and the lesson is how it works, three years before the Chinese Exclusion Act.

Connecting global issue

Politics, power and justice Language as an instrument of power — who wields it, who is spoken for, and who is silent.

Unique to each text

  • Culture, identity and community Othello — the outsider whose identity Venice assigns
  • Beliefs, values and education Malala — education as the thing a society chooses to pass on
  • Science, technology and the environment Thunberg — inherited environmental consequence
Q2

Readers, Writers, and Texts

Texts

  • The Hour of the Star — Lispector
  • Advertisements — Nike to Ad*Access
  • Infographics — Du Bois, Our World in Data
  • Satire — Swift, Twain, Key & Peele

Focus terms

  • metafiction
  • narrator intrusion
  • persona
  • irony
  • juxtaposition
  • aspirational address
  • target audience
  • axis and baseline
  • data framing

Genre characteristics

  • Metafictional novella — a narrator who shows you the writing as he does it
  • Advertisement — persuasion that must not look like persuasion
  • Data visualisation — argument disguised as description
  • Satirical essay / sketch — a persona whose composure is the real subject

How these texts connect

All four are about representing other people — for a public, and almost always for a purpose. Lispector’s narrator agonises openly about his right to write Macabéa. Advertisements represent an audience back to itself in order to sell. Infographics represent populations as shapes. Swift represents the Irish poor with appalling composure, and the composure is the point.

The strongest sequence in the course sits here: put Du Bois’s 1900 data portraits beside the Bellew cartoon from Q1. One is a white artist ventriloquising a Chinese voice; the other is a Black scholar seizing the same public instruments to represent his own people. Representation stops being an abstract term.

Connecting global issue

Politics, power and justice Who holds the pen — representation as an exercise of power over the people represented.

Unique to each text

  • Culture, identity and community Hour of the Star — class, region, and the invisibility of poverty
  • Art, creativity and the imagination Advertising — the manufactured consumer self
  • Science, technology and the environment Infographics — climate data and scientific literacy
Q3

Intertextuality / Review

Texts

  • Websites & blogs — NASA, NOAA, HONY, The Pudding
  • Review of all six works

Focus terms

  • hyperlink structure
  • navigation hierarchy
  • ethos by design
  • scroll rhetoric
  • user address
  • credibility markers
  • curation

Genre characteristics

  • Institutional website — authority built through structure, citation and restraint
  • Photo-and-caption blog — a stranger’s life delivered in a paragraph
  • Visual essay — data journalism descended directly from Du Bois

How these texts connect

The newest text type, used to re-read the whole course. Humans of New York is Lange with consent — portrait plus caption, ninety years later, with the subject speaking. NASA and NOAA build authority the way an institution does, through navigation and citation rather than voice. The Pudding is Du Bois’s descendant.

Useful closing question for the year: what has actually changed about representing a stranger? The technology, obviously. The ethics — that is the essay.

Connecting global issue

Science, technology and the environment How digital media construct authority, audience and identity online.

Unique to each text

  • Culture, identity and community Humans of New York — consent, voice, and the curated stranger
  • Art, creativity and the imagination The Pudding — visual essays that argue with data
Q4

Exam Preparation

Texts

  • All six works
  • All ten bodies of work
  • Paper 1 & Paper 2

Focus terms

  • guiding question
  • line of inquiry
  • comparative frame
  • textual evidence
  • text type conventions
  • authorial choice

Genre characteristics

  • Guided textual analysis — one unseen text, one guiding question, forty-five minutes
  • Comparative essay — two works, one question, an argument neither makes alone

How these texts connect

No new texts. The work of this quarter is retrieval and recombination — being able to reach for any of the six works and any of the ten bodies of work under time pressure.

The most common Paper 2 failure is treating comparison as alternation: a paragraph on one work, a paragraph on the other. Push students to find the problem two works share rather than the theme, then show how each solves it differently. Othello and The Hour of the Star both ask who is permitted to narrate a life — and answer in opposite directions.

Connecting global issue

Politics, power and justice All five fields are examinable — but every field, in the end, asks who has the power to make meaning.

Unique to each text

  • Culture, identity and community Identity and community across all six works
  • Beliefs, values and education Eliot and Malala — the narrowest field; know it well
  • Art, creativity and the imagination Creativity and form as arguments in their own right
