Question Banks

Questions that teach the moves, not the answers. Work down the ladder first, then use the bank for whichever concept, global issue or text type you are sitting with.

How to use these. Nobody arrives at a global issue by staring at a text. You climb to it. Start with the ladder below, which takes you from what is on the page to a statement you could open an Individual Oral with. The later banks are for when you are stuck on a particular concept, field, or text type.

Question Banks

How to think, not what to think
Name Period

Start with the ladder. The other tabs are for when you are stuck on a particular concept, field, text type or task.

The ladder — from text to global issue

Do these three steps in order. Most weak Individual Orals skip step 2 and land on a topic instead of an issue.

Step 1 — Start with the text comprehend

  1. What is literally happening, being shown, or being claimed?
  2. What text type is this, and what does that type conventionally do?
  3. List three specific features you could quote or point at.

Step 2 — Move to the idea analyse

  1. What is this text about beyond its subject?
  2. Which of the seven concepts does it most obviously engage? Which does it engage secretly?
  3. Name the tension the text will not resolve.

Step 3 — Reach the global issue evaluate

  1. Which field of inquiry does that tension belong to?
  2. State it as: ‘Today I’d like to discuss the field of … More specifically, …’
  3. Test it: is it big enough to matter beyond this text, and narrow enough to prove in ten minutes?
  4. Find a second text — one literary, one non-literary — where the same issue behaves differently.

By course concept

Seven concepts, each with the question that usually opens the text up.

Identity analyse

  1. Who is permitted to define this person — themselves, the narrator, or the audience?
  2. What does the text assume the reader already is? Who is the ‘we’ it addresses?
  3. Where does the character’s self-description contradict what the text shows us?
  4. If this identity were named differently — class instead of race, age instead of gender — what would stop working?

Culture analyse

  1. What does this text treat as normal and therefore never explains?
  2. Which values does it argue for openly, and which does it smuggle in as common sense?
  3. What would a reader from the text’s own moment notice that we miss — and the reverse?

Creativity evaluate

  1. What does this form let the maker do that another form could not?
  2. Where does the text break its own rules, and what does it buy by breaking them?
  3. Is the innovation in what is said, or in the order and shape of the saying?

Communication comprehend

  1. Who is speaking, to whom, and what do they want from them?
  2. What is the text’s first move to secure your trust? Does it earn it?
  3. What has been left out that the audience is expected to supply?

Perspective analyse

  1. Whose eyes are we behind, and what can that position not see?
  2. At what distance are we held from the subject — and who chose that distance?
  3. If the least powerful figure narrated instead, which sentence would have to change first?

Transformation evaluate

  1. What has this text taken from an earlier one, and what has it refused to take?
  2. Does the change flatter the original, argue with it, or replace it?
  3. What does the new audience need that the old one did not?

Representation analyse

  1. What is standing in for what — and who benefits from the substitution?
  2. Is this group being shown, or being spoken for? How can you tell?
  3. What does the text present as simply real that is in fact a choice of framing?

By field of inquiry

Use these once you know roughly which field you are in, to sharpen a topic into an issue.

Culture, identity and community analyse

  1. What does belonging cost the people in this text, and who sets the price?
  2. Which community is assumed, and who is standing just outside it?
  3. How does the text mark someone as an outsider before saying a word about them?

Beliefs, values and education analyse

  1. What does this society consider worth passing to the next generation?
  2. Where does a belief in this text survive the loss of the reason for it?
  3. Who is being educated, who is doing the educating, and what is the hidden syllabus?

Politics, power and justice evaluate

  1. Where is the power in this text actually located — not where it claims to be?
  2. Who is permitted to speak, who is spoken about, and who is silent?
  3. What injustice does the text expect you to already find normal?

Art, creativity and the imagination evaluate

  1. What is art asked to do in this text — console, provoke, sell, or record?
  2. Who decides what counts as art here, and who is excluded by that decision?
  3. What can this work say that a direct statement could not?

Science, technology and the environment analyse

  1. Is the environment a setting, a resource, or an agent in this text?
  2. What does technology promise here, and what does it quietly cost?
  3. Who bears the consequences of the change the text describes, and who is insulated from them?

By text type

Non-literary texts reward type-specific questions. These are the ones that tend to find the argument.

Music & lyrics comprehend

  1. Who is the ‘you’ in this song? Does it stay the same person throughout?
  2. What does the arrangement do that the words alone do not — and where do they disagree?
  3. Where does the repetition fall, and what does repeating it turn it into?

Photography analyse

  1. What is inside the frame, and what has been cut away to make the picture work?
  2. Did the subject consent to how they appear? What in the image tells you?
  3. Read the caption last: how much of what you ‘saw’ did the caption install?

Film analyse

  1. What does the camera do at the emotional peak — move closer, hold, or look away?
  2. Whose point of view does the editing privilege, and where does it withhold information?
  3. What does the sound tell you that the image contradicts?

Painting & visual art analyse

  1. Where does the light come from, and what does it refuse to reach?
  2. What is the distance between the figures, and what fills it?
  3. If you removed one object, would the meaning collapse? Which one, and why?

Advertising evaluate

  1. What problem does this ad invent so that the product can solve it?
  2. What kind of person are you being invited to become?
  3. What is the smallest true claim being made, underneath the largest implied one?

Infographics & data evaluate

  1. Where does the axis start, and what would change if it started at zero?
  2. What has been counted, and what could not be counted and so was dropped?
  3. Who made this, who paid for it, and what would count as evidence against it?

Satire evaluate

  1. Who is the persona, and where does the writer first let the mask slip?
  2. What is the real target — the person named, or the audience that tolerates them?
  3. Who is not laughing, and is that a flaw in the satire or the point of it?

Speeches analyse

  1. What does the opening sentence do to the room before any argument arrives?
  2. Where does the speaker claim authority — from position, suffering, or evidence?
  3. Which sentence would be quoted, and was it built to be quoted?

Political cartoons analyse

  1. What must you already know for this to be legible? What does that tell you about the audience?
  2. Which figure is drawn with the most exaggeration, and what does the exaggeration argue?
  3. Where is the caricature doing the work the caption cannot admit to?

Websites & blogs analyse

  1. What does the navigation put first, and what does it bury?
  2. How does the site build credibility — citation, design, tone, or sheer volume?
  3. Who is the imagined visitor, and what are they meant to do before they leave?

By assessment

What each component actually rewards, phrased as questions to ask yourself while preparing.

Paper 1 — unseen non-literary text analyse

  1. In sixty seconds: what type of text, what audience, what does it want?
  2. What is the single most unusual formal choice on the page? Build the essay from it.
  3. What does the text do at its start and its end — and why that order?
  4. What is your guiding question going to be, and can you answer it with evidence you can see?

Paper 2 — comparative essay evaluate

  1. What do these two works genuinely share — not theme, but problem?
  2. Where do they diverge in method while agreeing in conclusion, or the reverse?
  3. Which work resists the question you have been asked, and can you say so intelligently?
  4. What does reading them together reveal that neither shows alone?

Individual Oral create

  1. Can you state your global issue in two sentences without naming either text?
  2. Which extract, of forty lines, contains the most of your argument?
  3. How does the non-literary text complicate — not merely echo — the literary one?
  4. What is the strongest objection to your reading, and what is your answer to it?

HL Essay create

  1. What is your line of inquiry, phrased as a question you do not yet know the answer to?
  2. Why does this question need 1,200–1,500 words — what makes it non-obvious?
  3. Which secondary idea (theory, context, critical lens) earns its place, and which is decoration?
