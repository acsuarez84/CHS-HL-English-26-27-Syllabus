Course overview

In this course, students study a wide range of literary and non-literary texts in a variety of media. By examining communicative acts across literary form and textual type alongside appropriate secondary readings, students will investigate the nature of language itself and the ways in which it shapes and is influenced by identity and culture. Approaches to study in the course are wide-ranging and can include literary theory, sociolinguistics, media studies, and critical discourse analysis.

IB aim The complex and dynamic nature of language and both its practical and aesthetic dimensions.

Language is never neutral — and it is always changing.

What that means in practice

  • Notice that a word choice is a choice, with alternatives that were rejected.
  • Separate what language does practically (informs, sells, instructs) from what it does aesthetically (moves, unsettles, delights) — and see texts doing both at once.
  • Track how meaning shifts with time: a 1944 advertisement reads differently now than it did then.

Where you do it Every unit, but most sharply in the advertising and satire units, where the practical and the aesthetic pull against each other.

IB aim The crucial role language plays in communication, reflecting experience, and shaping the world.

Language does not just describe the world. It builds it.

What that means in practice

  • See how naming something changes how it is treated — in law, in advertising, in a play.
  • Watch a speech move a room, and analyse the machinery that let it.
  • Recognise when a text is reflecting an experience and when it is manufacturing one.

Where you do it Year 2 Q1, where Othello, five speeches and the political cartoons sit together and the same rhetorical moves serve opposite ends.

IB aim Their own roles as producers of language, developing productive skills.

You are not only reading these texts. You are making your own.

What that means in practice

  • Write analytically under time (Paper 1, Paper 2) and at length with revision (HL Essay).
  • Speak analytically for ten minutes and defend it under questioning (Individual Oral).
  • Build a line of inquiry that is genuinely yours — open enough to argue, narrow enough to finish.

Where you do it Continuously. The Learner Portfolio runs all year; the HL Essay is drafted across the summer and first half of senior year.

IB aim How language choices, text types, literary forms, and contextual elements affect meaning.

Form is not decoration. It is part of the argument.

What that means in practice

  • Name what you are looking at — a soliloquy, an infographic, a documentary photograph — and know what that form conventionally does.
  • Explain how a choice within the form (a cropped edge, a moved baseline, an enjambed line) changes what the text can mean.
  • Use the right vocabulary precisely: the focus terms on each quarter card.

Where you do it Every quarter card carries the genre elements for that quarter’s text types — 102 defined terms across the two years.

IB aim Their own interpretations and the critical perspectives of others, exploring how positions are shaped by cultural belief systems.

Your reading has a standpoint. So does everyone else’s.

What that means in practice

  • Defend an interpretation with evidence, and change it when the evidence says so.
  • Take seriously a reading you disagree with, and say precisely where it parts from yours.
  • Ask what a text assumes its reader already believes — and whether you do.

Where you do it Class discussion throughout, and formally in Paper 2, where two works must be held in view at once.

IB aim How texts and their visual and audio elements work together to influence audiences.

Most texts you will meet are not words alone.

What that means in practice

  • Read an image as carefully as a sentence: framing, cropping, light, what sits outside the frame.
  • Notice when sound and image disagree in a film, and ask what the disagreement argues.
  • Analyse how a website’s navigation and design build authority before you read a word of it.

Where you do it Year 1 Q2 (Lange and Interstellar), Q3 (Hopper), and Year 2 Q3 (websites and blogs).

Aim 1 of 6

Every text in this course is mapped. Each of the six literary works and each non-literary body of work connects to at least one IB course concept and one Global Issue field of inquiry. That mapping is what makes the Individual Oral possible — each task needs a non-literary text and a literary work that share a global issue.
