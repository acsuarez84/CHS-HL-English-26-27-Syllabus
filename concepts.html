Concepts & Global Issues

Every literary work and non-literary text connects to at least one IB course concept and one Global Issue field of inquiry.

The difference that trips people up

A field of inquiry is one of five broad areas — think of them as shelves. A global issue is the narrow, arguable claim you take off that shelf. A line of inquiry is the question you build an HL Essay around.

Field → Politics, power and justice  →  Global issue → how a society can need someone and still refuse to accept them  →  Line of inquiry → how Shakespeare lets other characters describe Othello before he speaks for himself.

Each field tab below shows that same walk, worked through with a text from this course.

Concepts & Fields

7 concepts · 5 fields of inquiry
Name Period

The first tab holds the seven course concepts. The five that follow take one field of inquiry each, in plain language, with a worked example of narrowing it into a line of inquiry.

The seven course concepts

Each concept lists the works and non-literary texts that carry it, and the through-line that links them. Use these when choosing texts for the Individual Oral or an HL Essay line of inquiry, and the question bank to open them up.

Identity

How texts construct, perform, and challenge personal, cultural, and national identities.

Works that carry it

  • The Tunnel — Castel’s self exists only in relation to a woman he cannot know
  • The Hour of the Star — Macabéa barely registers as a person, even to the narrator
  • Othello — an identity Venice assigns before Othello can claim it
  • Selected Poems — the self dispersed across Prufrock, Gerontion, the Magi
  • The Woman in the Dunes — Niki loses a name and acquires a function
  • Mansfield — Miss Brill sees herself as a character in a play, until someone says otherwise

Non-literary texts

  • Dove Self-Esteem Project
  • Nike, ‘Purpose’
  • Humans of New York
  • Tracy Chapman, ‘Fast Car’

How they connect. In every literary work, identity is under pressure from something external — class, race, marriage, captivity, the city. In the non-literary texts identity is being sold (Dove, Nike) or curated (Humans of New York). Put them together and the question sharpens: is identity something you have, or something done to you?

Culture

The social, historical, and ideological contexts that shape and are shaped by texts.

Works that carry it

  • The Hour of the Star — the Northeast arriving in Rio and not surviving it
  • Othello — Venice’s rules, and what happens to the man who is useful but not belonging
  • Mansfield — the small cruelties a class system performs politely

Non-literary texts

  • Ad*Access archive, 1911–1955
  • Edward Hopper
  • Bellew, ‘Melican Leportee Man’
  • Tracy Chapman

How they connect. Culture is what a text never bothers to explain. The Ad*Access advertisements are the clearest teaching instrument here, because a 1944 ad has to state its assumptions out loud to sell anything — assumptions Mansfield and Shakespeare leave silent.

Creativity

The artistic choices and innovation of writers and producers across literary and non-literary forms.

Works that carry it

  • The Hour of the Star — a narrator who shows you the writing as he does it
  • Selected Poems — modernist form as an argument about a broken world

Non-literary texts

  • LEGO, ‘About Us’
  • Du Bois, Data Portraits
  • The Pudding
  • Hopper

How they connect. Two questions run through these: what can a form do that no other form can, and who is allowed to be called creative? Du Bois answers the second by force — making beauty out of census data because the argument would not be heard otherwise.

Communication

How texts construct meaning between producer and audience.

Works that carry it

  • The Tunnel — a novel about the impossibility of being understood
  • Mansfield — what characters cannot say to each other

Non-literary texts

  • All five speeches
  • Infographics
  • NASA and NOAA
  • Advertising

How they connect. The literary works are studies in communication failing; the non-literary texts are studies in it succeeding, sometimes on false terms. Reading Douglass beside The Tunnel shows the same instrument used to build a bridge and to prove none is possible.

Perspective

How point of view, voice, and standpoint shape interpretation.

Works that carry it

  • The Tunnel — an unreliable narrator who tells you so on page one
  • Othello — every character sees a different Othello
  • Mansfield — free indirect discourse: whose sentence is this?
  • The Woman in the Dunes — the outsider’s certainty eroding

Non-literary texts

  • Dorothea Lange
  • Political cartoons
  • Satire
  • Key & Peele

How they connect. Perspective is the course’s most transferable concept, because it is the one Paper 1 tests hardest. Lange and the cartoonists both frame a person for an audience; the difference is whether the framing admits itself.

Transformation

How texts adapt, rewrite, and respond to other texts and contexts.

Works that carry it

  • Othello — noble general to murderer in five acts
  • Selected Poems — spiritual change in ‘Journey of the Magi’ and ‘Marina’
  • The Woman in the Dunes — a man who stops wanting to leave

Non-literary texts

  • Speeches as instruments of change
  • Ad*Access, 1911–1955
  • Satire

How they connect. Ask in each case who is doing the transforming. Othello is transformed by Iago; the Magi transform themselves; Niki is transformed by sand and habit. That distinction is often the whole of a good HL Essay.

Representation

How language and form (re)present reality, identities, and ideas.

Works that carry it

  • The Hour of the Star — the ethics of writing about the poor, made explicit
  • Othello — a Moor written for an audience that had met few
  • Mansfield — class and ageing in miniature

Non-literary texts

  • Dorothea Lange
  • Du Bois, Data Portraits
  • Bellew cartoon
  • Ad*Access
  • Infographics

How they connect. This is the concept where the course’s strongest pairing lives. Bellew ventriloquises a Chinese voice for a white readership; Du Bois, twenty years later, seizes the same public instruments to represent his own people. Put them side by side and representation stops being an abstract term.

Culture, identity and community

This field is about belonging — who is let in, who is kept out, and what it costs to be either. It covers family, class, race, ethnicity, nationality and religion, and what happens when people move between communities.

Questions this field asks

  • Who decides whether this person belongs?
  • What does the text treat as normal, and who does that normal exclude?
  • What does a character have to hide, perform or give up in order to be accepted?

From topic to line of inquiry

Too broad“Identity in The Hour of the Star.”
Getting there“How poverty makes Macabéa invisible.”
A line of inquiry“How Lispector uses a narrator who cannot stop apologising to show that even sympathetic representation can erase the person it describes.”

IB wording How texts explore family, class, race, ethnicity, nationality and religion, and how these shape individuals and societies — including migration, colonialism and nationalism.

Literary works (primary): The Tunnel, The Hour of the Star, Selected Short Stories

Non-literary texts available for this field (11)

  • Dorothea Lange — full photo essay
  • Edward Hopper — Automat, Morning Sun
  • Edward Hopper — Nighthawks, Early Sunday Morning
  • Frank Bellew, “Melican Leportee Man” (1879)
  • Humans of New York
  • Key & Peele, “Obama’s Anger Translator”

  • Sojourner Truth, “Ain’t I a Woman?” (1851)
  • Tracy Chapman — “Behind the Wall”

Beliefs, values and education

This field is about what a society holds true and teaches to the next generation — faith, values, morality, and education itself. It is also about what happens when those beliefs collide, or when someone stops believing.

Questions this field asks

  • What does this text assume is right, and never argue for?
  • What is being passed on here — and who is excluded from receiving it?
  • What survives when the belief behind it has gone?

From topic to line of inquiry

Too broad“Religion in Eliot’s poetry.”
Getting there“Eliot’s speakers want faith but cannot reach it.”
A line of inquiry“How Eliot uses fragments of prayer and scripture inside secular scenes to show a culture keeping the shape of belief after losing its content.”

IB wording How texts explore the beliefs and values nurtured in particular societies, how they shape people and educational systems, and the tensions when beliefs conflict.

Literary works (primary): Selected Poems

Non-literary texts available for this field (1)
  • Malala Yousafzai, UN Youth Assembly (2013)

Politics, power and justice

This field is about power — who holds it, how they got it, how it is used, and how people resist it. It covers rights, law, government, wealth and inequality, and it is the field most global issues end up touching.

Questions this field asks

  • Who has power in this text, and where does it actually come from?
  • Who is allowed to speak, who is spoken about, and who is silent?
  • What injustice does the text expect you to accept without noticing?

From topic to line of inquiry

Too broad“Racism in Othello.”
Getting there“Venice needs Othello but never accepts him.”
A line of inquiry“How Shakespeare has other characters narrate Othello before he speaks, so that his identity arrives already written by the people who exclude him.”

IB wording How texts explore rights and responsibilities, government and institutions, hierarchies of power, the distribution of wealth, the limits of law, equality and human rights.

Literary works (primary): Othello

Non-literary texts available for this field (6)
  • “The Rich Growing Richer…” (1871)
  • Dorothea Lange — Damaged Child, Migrant Mother
  • Frederick Douglass, “What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?” (1852)
  • Martin Luther King Jr., “I Have a Dream” (1963)
  • Swift, “A Modest Proposal” (1729)
  • W. E. B. Du Bois — Data Portraits (1900)

Art, creativity and the imagination

This field is about what art is for — how it is made, what it does to the people who encounter it, and who gets to decide what counts as art at all. It includes beauty, craft, imagination, and the ways art can challenge how we see.

Questions this field asks

  • What can this form do that no other form could?
  • What is art asked to do here — console, provoke, sell, or record?
  • Who is allowed to be called an artist, and who is left out of that word?

From topic to line of inquiry

Too broad“Creativity in advertising.”
Getting there“Advertisements borrow the techniques of art.”
A line of inquiry“How the LEGO brand site uses the visual language of childhood imagination to make buying a product feel like an act of creativity.”

IB wording How texts explore aesthetic inspiration, craft and beauty — and the function, value and effects of art in society.

Literary works (primary): none as primary

Non-literary texts available for this field (0)

    Science, technology and the environment

    This field is about people, technology and the natural world — how humans shape the environment and are shaped by it, what technology promises and what it costs, and who carries the consequences of both.

    Questions this field asks

    • Is the environment the setting here, a resource, or an agent with its own force?
    • What does the technology promise, and what does it quietly take?
    • Who suffers the consequence, and who is protected from it?

    From topic to line of inquiry

    Too broad“Nature in The Woman in the Dunes.”
    Getting there“The sand controls the villagers’ lives.”
    A line of inquiry“How Abe makes sand behave like an institution — patient, impersonal and impossible to argue with — so that surviving nature and obeying a society become the same act.”

    IB wording How texts explore the relationship between humans and the environment, and the implications of technology and media for society.

    Literary works (primary): The Woman in the Dunes

    Non-literary texts available for this field (4)
    • Interstellar (Nolan, 2014)
    • Greta Thunberg, UN Climate Action Summit (2019)
    • NASA & NOAA climate sites
    • Our World in Data · climate infographics
