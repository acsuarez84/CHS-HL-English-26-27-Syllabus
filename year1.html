Combined texts, quarter by quarter

What to watch for in each quarter — the vocabulary to master, the genre moves to name, and the questions to hold while you read. Laid out as Cornell notes so you can mirror the format in your own notebook.

First, a definition: what is a field of inquiry?

A field of inquiry is one of five broad areas the IB uses to sort the big human questions that texts raise. Think of them as the five shelves any global issue can sit on:

  • Culture, identity and community who belongs, who is excluded, and what identity costs
  • Beliefs, values and education what a society holds true and passes on
  • Politics, power and justice who has power, and how it is used or resisted
  • Art, creativity and the imagination what art is for, and who decides
  • Science, technology and the environment people, technology, and the natural world

A field is not your global issue — it is far too big for that. Your global issue is a narrow, arguable claim you carve out of a field: it must matter beyond one text, be visible in the text itself, and be provable in ten minutes.

Field: Politics, power and justice.   Too broad: “racism in Othello.”   A global issue: “how a society lets an outsider succeed while never letting him belong, so that his identity stays something others assign him.”

The Individual Oral opens by naming your field, then narrowing to your issue. Practise that move all year — see the ladder in the question banks.

Q1

Readers, Writers, and Texts

Texts

  • The Tunnel — Sabato
  • Tracy Chapman, Tracy Chapman

Focus terms & genre elements

Prose fiction
narrator
the voice telling the story — never assume it is the author
unreliable narrator
a narrator whose account we have reason to doubt
retrospective narration
telling it afterwards, when the ending is already known
first-person
the ‘I’ voice; we see only what that person sees
motif
an image or idea that keeps returning and gathers meaning
characterisation
how a text builds a person — through speech, action, others’ views
Song lyrics
persona
the invented speaker of a song; not the singer’s own self
refrain
a repeated line; repetition changes what it means
second-person address
speaking to ‘you’ — who is that, and can they reply?
register
how formal or casual the language is, and what that signals
diction
word choice, and the world those words come from
imagery
language that appeals to the senses

Genre characteristics

  • Confessional novella — a narrator writing from prison, controlling every fact we receive
  • Narrative ballad — a singer-songwriter telling someone else’s life in first person

Hold these questions while you read

  1. Castel tells you he is a murderer in his first sentence. Why begin there? What does that opening buy him for the rest of the book?
  2. Mark every point where Castel addresses you directly. What is he asking you to do?
  3. Keep a running list of what Castel claims to know about María. Beside each item, note how he knows it. How much survives?
  4. In “Fast Car,” who is ‘you’? Does the same person stay in that position across all four verses?
  5. Both texts withhold names, dates, or places. Find three omissions. What does each one accomplish that stating the fact would not?
  6. Chapman’s narrator plans an escape; Castel narrates from a cell. Which of them is more trapped, and what in the text makes you say so?

Working toward your global issue

  1. Both texts are addressed to someone who never answers. What does speaking into silence do to the story a person tells about themselves?
  2. Whose account of a life do you actually trust here — and on what grounds?
  3. Which field of inquiry does that question belong to? Write one sentence naming it, then narrow it until you could argue it in ten minutes.
Q2

Readers, Writers, and Texts

Texts

  • Dorothea Lange — 6 photographs
  • T. S. Eliot — Selected Poems
  • Interstellar — Nolan

Focus terms & genre elements

Photography
framing
what the photographer chose to include
cropping
what was cut away — always ask what sits outside
composition
how elements are arranged inside the frame
focal point
where the eye lands first, and how it was pulled there
caption
the words attached to an image; they instruct how to read it
depth of field
what is sharp and what is blurred, and why
Poetry
metaphor
one thing described as another, so its qualities transfer
image
a picture made in words; the basic unit of a poem
objective correlative
Eliot’s own term: an object or scene that stands in for a feeling
allusion
a reference to another text, expecting you to catch it
fragmentation
a poem broken into pieces that do not smoothly join
enjambment
a line running past its break into the next
free verse
poetry without regular metre or rhyme
juxtaposition
two things placed side by side so each comments on the other
Film
mise-en-scène
everything arranged inside the shot — set, light, costume, placement
screen space
how much of the frame a figure occupies, and how much emptiness surrounds them
shot distance
close-up, mid, wide — how near the camera lets us stand
montage
meaning built by cutting shots together
sound design
score, silence and effects, and where they contradict the image
lighting
high or low key; where shadow falls

Genre characteristics

  • Documentary photograph — a composed image presented as neutral record
  • Modernist lyric — fragmented, allusive, refusing to resolve
  • Science-fiction film — spectacle carrying an argument about survival

Hold these questions while you read

  1. For each Lange photograph: what is inside the frame, and what had to be cut away to make the picture work? Sketch what you think sits just outside it.
  2. Read each caption last. How much of what you thought you saw was installed by the words?
  3. In Eliot, mark every image of dust, ash, dryness or stone. What accumulates by the end?
  4. Eliot’s speaker changes without announcement. Find a place where it happens — how did you know?
  5. In Interstellar, what does the camera do at each emotional peak: move closer, hold, or look away? What does that choice argue?
  6. Lange and Eliot were working in the same two decades. What does each one notice about that period that the other cannot?

Working toward your global issue

  1. In all three texts something in the land or the air has stopped sustaining people. Is the environment the subject here, or the instrument?
  2. Who bears the consequence in each text, and who is insulated from it?
  3. Name the field of inquiry. Then narrow it until it is specific enough to prove with evidence you can point at.
Q3

Time & Space

Texts

  • The Woman in the Dunes — Abe
  • Edward Hopper — 6 paintings

Focus terms & genre elements

Prose fiction
allegory
a story whose surface stands for something larger
symbol
an object that carries meaning beyond itself
setting as agent
when place acts on characters rather than just surrounding them
repetition
recurrence used structurally — here, the endless shovelling
pacing
how fast time moves, and where the text slows down
threshold
a boundary a character can or cannot cross
Painting
composition
how the canvas is organised; what leads the eye
light source
where light enters, and what it leaves dark
negative space
the emptiness between and around figures — it carries meaning
perspective
the viewer’s implied position; where the painting puts you
colour palette
the range of colours chosen, and the mood it builds
framing device
a window, door or edge that frames the subject within the picture

Genre characteristics

  • Existentialist novel — a realistic surface over an allegorical structure
  • Realist painting — an ordinary interior arranged to produce a feeling

Hold these questions while you read

  1. In each Hopper painting: where does the light come from, and what does it refuse to reach?
  2. Measure the distance between figures in Room in New York and Nighthawks. What fills that space?
  3. Track every time Niki plans to escape. What has changed between the first plan and the last?
  4. What can the sand do that a human antagonist could not?
  5. Find the sentence where the pit stops being a prison. Can you defend your choice?
  6. Both a painting and a novel put a person inside a container. Which medium makes you feel the confinement more — and what does the other one gain in exchange?

Working toward your global issue

  1. Each text ends with someone who could leave and does not. Is that defeat, adaptation, or something the text refuses to settle?
  2. Who built the structure each figure sits inside — and does the text let you see them?
  3. Name the field of inquiry, then narrow it. Test it: would it still matter to someone who had never read this book?
Q4

Intertextuality

Texts

  • Katherine Mansfield — 6 short stories
  • Individual Oral

Focus terms & genre elements

Short fiction
epiphany
a sudden moment of seeing that reframes a whole life
free indirect discourse
narrator’s words and character’s thoughts in the same sentence
focalisation
whose consciousness we are inside at any moment
closure
how an ending settles — or refuses to
irony
a gap between what is said and what is meant, or expected and actual
class marker
a detail — object, accent, food — that places someone socially
compression
a whole life implied in a few pages

Genre characteristics

  • Modernist short story — a life compressed into a few pages and one moment of seeing

Hold these questions while you read

  1. Paragraph by paragraph, ask: whose sentence is this? The narrator’s, the character’s, or both at once?
  2. Mark the moment each story turns. How many sentences does the turn actually take?
  3. What does each woman believe about herself that the story quietly contradicts?
  4. List the class markers on the first page — objects, speech, clothing, food. How fast does Mansfield place someone socially?
  5. Which earlier Year 1 text does each story most resemble? Be able to defend the comparison in one sentence.
  6. Where does a story end a beat earlier than you expected? What does the withheld ending do?

Working toward your global issue

  1. Mansfield’s women are constrained by something none of them can name. Can you name it?
  2. Draft your Individual Oral global issue statement. Then cut it in half and see whether it got stronger.
  3. Find your second text — one literary, one non-literary. Where does the same issue behave differently in the second one? That difference is your argument.
