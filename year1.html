Combined texts, quarter by quarter

How the literary and non-literary texts in each quarter speak to each other — the terms to master, the genre moves to name, the global issue they share, and the ones each text carries alone. Laid out as Cornell notes so you can mirror the format in your own notebook.

Q1

Readers, Writers, and Texts

Texts

  • The Tunnel — Sabato
  • Tracy Chapman, Tracy Chapman

Focus terms

  • unreliable narrator
  • confession
  • retrospective narration
  • persona
  • second-person address
  • register
  • refrain
  • diction

Genre characteristics

  • Confessional novella — a narrator writing from prison, controlling every fact we receive
  • Narrative ballad — a singer-songwriter telling someone else’s life in first person

How these texts connect

Both are voices speaking to someone who cannot answer. Castel writes his confession to a reader he will never meet; Chapman’s narrators address a ‘you’ who never replies — the partner in “Fast Car,” the neighbour who hears everything in “Behind the Wall.” In both, the act of telling is a substitute for being heard.

Ask students to notice what each voice withholds. Castel admits to murder in his first sentence and spends the book justifying it. Chapman never names the abuser, the city, or the year — and the omission is the argument.

Connecting global issue

Culture, identity and community Isolation and the failure to be known — a person narrating a life that no one is listening to.

Unique to each text

  • Beliefs, values and education The Tunnel — existentialist despair, meaning as a private invention
  • Politics, power and justice Chapman — poverty, domestic violence, the economics of escape
Q2

Readers, Writers, and Texts

Texts

  • Dorothea Lange — 6 photographs
  • T. S. Eliot — Selected Poems
  • Interstellar — Nolan

Focus terms

  • framing
  • cropping
  • caption
  • fragment
  • allusion
  • objective correlative
  • juxtaposition
  • mise-en-scène
  • montage
  • imagery of dust and waste

Genre characteristics

  • Documentary photograph — a composed image presented as neutral record
  • Modernist lyric — fragmented, allusive, refusing to resolve
  • Science-fiction film — spectacle carrying an argument about survival

How these texts connect

Three ways of looking at a wasteland. Lange photographs land that has stopped feeding people; Eliot writes a city emptied of meaning; Nolan films a planet that has given up on its inhabitants. In all three the environment carries the emotional content — dust, ash, and blight do the work that a stated feeling would do in a lesser text.

The pairing to press: Lange’s photographs and Eliot’s poems were made in the same two decades. One looks outward at a country in crisis; the other inward at a self coming apart. Ask which is the more honest account of the 1930s — and how each one frames to get there.

Connecting global issue

Science, technology and the environment Land and atmosphere that no longer sustain human life — environment as the condition, not the backdrop.

Unique to each text

  • Politics, power and justice Lange — the ethics of documentary witness; who is composed for whom
  • Beliefs, values and education Eliot — spiritual crisis; tradition emptying out
  • Art, creativity and the imagination Interstellar — spectacle as a way of arguing
Q3

Time & Space

Texts

  • The Woman in the Dunes — Abe
  • Edward Hopper — 6 paintings

Focus terms

  • allegory
  • symbol
  • setting as agent
  • confinement
  • repetition
  • threshold
  • negative space
  • light source
  • composition
  • perspective

Genre characteristics

  • Existentialist novel — a realistic surface over an allegorical structure
  • Realist painting — an ordinary interior arranged to produce a feeling

How these texts connect

Both are about people held inside a space — and both make architecture do the psychological work. Niki is trapped in a sand pit; Hopper’s figures sit in rooms with windows they do not look through. Neither text explains the feeling; both build a container and let the reader infer it.

Watch what light does. Hopper’s light always arrives from outside the frame and reaches only part of the room. Abe’s sand is always falling, always needing to be shovelled. Both are studies in repetition as a condition of life — and both end ambiguously, with a character who could leave and does not.

Connecting global issue

Culture, identity and community The self enclosed by a structure it did not choose — isolation as architecture rather than accident.

Unique to each text

  • Science, technology and the environment Dunes — sand as an agent with its own will
  • Art, creativity and the imagination Hopper — the American mid-century imagination; what painting can hold
Q4

Intertextuality

Texts

  • Katherine Mansfield — 6 short stories
  • Individual Oral

Focus terms

  • epiphany
  • free indirect discourse
  • closure
  • class marker
  • irony
  • focalisation
  • the modernist miniature

Genre characteristics

  • Modernist short story — a life compressed into a few pages and one moment of seeing

How these texts connect

This is the synthesis quarter, and Mansfield is the right text for it because her stories rehearse everything Year 1 has done. Miss Brill’s self-deception is Castel’s, at a smaller scale. The composed public self is Lange’s framing. The constrained life is Chapman’s. The room that will not release its occupant is Hopper’s.

Free indirect discourse is the term to nail down here: the narrator’s sentence and the character’s thought occupying the same words. Once students can hear it, they can hear perspective everywhere — which is exactly what the IO and Paper 1 reward.

Connecting global issue

Culture, identity and community Loneliness and constrained lives — the small social cruelties a class system performs politely.

Unique to each text

  • Politics, power and justice Mansfield — gender and servitude; who is paid to be invisible
